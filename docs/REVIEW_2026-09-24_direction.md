## 1. The verdict

**Achievable, in a bounded form.** The game's own developer (DeNA) built a reinforcement-learning bot that plays Pocket without seeing hidden cards, drives the in-game CPU opponents and runs automated battles with a player's deck across about 540 preset decks. It took a 2-million-parameter Transformer fed card information, 50 million games per model, and six 32-core machines, and still needed hand fixes for plays it got wrong (one card pattern's correct-use rate rose to 96.2% after adjustments; no human-strength claim was made). So the ceiling exists. Nobody has shown it at hobby scale, and "near human level" has a cost the project has not yet paid: no human has ever played this bot.

What "achievable" means here, concretely:
- A pilot that plays every deck the engine implements at one consistent, competent level, so that bot-vs-bot matchup tables land within a few points of real results and a brew's screen result means something. This is reachable on the laptop in weeks with the tools already in the repo, mainly search plus a better position score. The evidence for that is in section 2.
- A pilot at the level of a strong tournament player, on brand-new cards the week they release: not reachable, because the cards must be implemented and verified in the engine before any bot can play them, and not needed for the goal. Compute alone is not the barrier to a general learned pilot: DeNA's 50 million games per model is about 85 hours at this laptop's 163 self-play games per second (about 8 days at run 4's 72 games per second with training), so the constraint on a run 6-style bot is the card encoding and the training design, not the machine (section 7). New cards have to be added to the engine and verified before the bot can play them. B4b (Sept 29 to 30) is mostly reprints plus about five new cards, a one-to-three-day data refresh; the real treadmill is the next series, C1, around Oct 28, and the fork has never merged upstream code since July 30 (it imports card data and implements mechanics itself).

**Direction: right since Sept 10, with one wrong turn.** The two decisions that mattered were fixing the rules first and making the Limitless table the scoreboard. The rules fixes cut the scoreboard's real error from 11.8 to 8.8 points (the correlation moved 0.55 to 0.68, which on its own is within noise). Everything else measured since then was a small or zero gain except one thing: giving the search a guess at the opponent's hidden cards (option B), and tonight's attribution shows why that worked (section 8). The wrong turn is treating another network run (run 6) as the long bet. Five training runs of the same family have shown what it can and cannot do so far; what none has shown is a network piloting a deck it was not trained on, and testing that properly costs a full run.

**Efficiency: the waste is not laptop time, it is review layers and bookkeeping around diagnostics, and the missing direct test.** Sept 18 to 24 is the most productive week the project has had, and it still spent three Astra review rounds and a lead review on the seed guards of a practice-run launcher. Diagnostics that decide nothing on the plan page should not be built, and diagnostics that do should get one agent and one results file, not a review chain.

What the bot is, from the code, in one paragraph: k3 looks up to three of its own actions ahead inside its turn, never into the opponent's turn, and scores the position with a linear formula of thirteen weights, eleven live, set by hand as round numbers (a point 10,000, a sure win 100,000, turns until the opponent wins 100, the Active's readiness 500, HP times energy plus one 1, hand and deck sizes 1, own retreat cost −1, Active safety 1, a Tool 10, the opponent's discard 0.1). The k tier was picked among tiers by its correlation with real results, but no weight was ever fitted; the formula has no Weakness term at all, and its damage estimate falls back to printed damage. Any line that would need to know an unknown opponent card is cut off and scored by that formula, and any effect whose text names the opponent's hand or deck is left unpriced altogether while those zones hold unknown cards. The reply-search tiers (x, y, s) use a different leaf, and under closed information their reply search never runs past the opponent's first draw; the "same games" figure (2,440 of 2,500) belongs to the cloud patch kr3, whose +16 on Hydreigon v Lucario came with a full-hand reply that the repo's public-only reply does not have. The b tier samples the opponent's unseen cards from their list, runs k3's search in each sampled world and averages. There is no learned position score anywhere in the engine, and the player called MCTS plays random moves in real games.

Why learning has not paid off here is also plain from the files. The networks learn only from who won. A 20-damage attack that does not change who wins produces no training signal at all (the feasibility file says this itself). They are keyed to card numbers, so a new deck is a new problem. And they never search: run 3's networks missed 5 to 15% of sure knockouts (stage 1's Weezing did not, 4.5% against k3's 6.5%) and they bench less than k3 even when they beat it. Meanwhile the one lever that moved the worst cell by 12 to 15 points in a day was letting the search price cards it had been leaving unpriced, a fix that names no card and needs no training. Search plays any deck the engine implements without training; its blind spots are mechanism-shaped, not deck-shaped, which is what makes them fixable once.

## 3. What to do next, in order (superseded)

This section was Fable's first draft, written before the exchange with the laptop session. **It is superseded by section 8**, the consensus list, which keeps its shape but changes the objective, the order, the reading rules and several steps. It stays here for the record.

Each step names the decision it makes, its cost, and when to stop.

1. **Read tonight's option B table and name the pilot candidate.** Decision: does b3o3n4 beat k3 on the scoreboard (correlation, average miss, cells beyond chance, favorites right)? If yes, it is the candidate; it becomes the default for every table and for the brew screen once step 2(b) confirms the gain survives the fixes below. Cost: nothing, it finishes tonight. Watch for cells that move the wrong way (Altaria v Hydreigon went from 57.8 to 46.7 in the first rows, real 57.0).
2. **Fix the b player, re-run the five worst cells, then find the cheapest variant that keeps the gain.** Added 23:30 from the laptop session's read-only review of the b player (two verifiers, no hidden-information leak found): plain b with o0 never consults k3's certified-lethal-reply check, because no slot is Unknown once the guess fills them, so b3n4's +12 on Hydreigon v Lucario may be partly recklessness rather than information; the sampler ignores revealed opponent deck membership; n above 1 also averages over the bot's own deck order and the search's randomness; o above 0 also switches on the consistent horizon and public-only forced choices. So, in order: (a) make b consult the lethal-reply check when o=0, or apply it inside each sampled world, and make the sampler respect revealed deck membership; (b) re-run the five worst cells at 500 deals with the fixed b3o3n4 and b3n4 against tonight's numbers, and see how much of the gain survives; (c) only then compare variants one change at a time: one guess or four (n1 vs n4), reply search on or off (o0 vs o3), hard-minimum reply or averaged reply (the see-everything test showed the hard minimum makes a bot timid when it sees more). Cost: about two laptop hours plus the small code fix. Decision: which variant runs every table from now on. Stop: once a variant is within noise of the fixed b3o3n4 at half the cost or less. A cloud first look at b3n1 and b3o3n1 started at 18:00 on a branch; it is a first look only and is read with these confounds beside its numbers.
3. **Dustin plays the bot.** Twenty games in the engine's human seat (`--players h,<bot>` in WSL), both sides on meta lists, then swap. He notes every move he thinks is wrong. Cost: one evening, plus about an hour of agent work first to make the printed board readable. Decision: is this bot near his level or not, which is the question this project exists to answer, and it produces the blind-spot list that step 4 works from. The Hyper Ray blind spot was found by Dustin reading five transcripts; twenty games will find more.
4. **Fix blind spots one at a time in the position score or the search rule.** Each fix gets a paired-seed A/B on the cells it should affect (1,000 games, read only differences over 5 points), then the table is re-scored. Cost: small Rust changes, an hour or two of laptop time each. Stop: when the table meets the target (about 6 points average miss, 3 or 4 cells beyond chance) or when Dustin's list is empty.
5. **Only if 4 stalls: tune the thirteen weights for self-play strength** with a black-box optimizer over the eight lists with a held-out deck, the way the deckgym-evalbot fork did (63% head to head over its hand-set baseline). Never tune to the Limitless cells directly; the table stays the external check. Cost: a few laptop hours per pass. Note from that fork: tuned weights are specific to a search depth.
6. **Only if 4 and 5 stall: a learned position score inside the search.** Trained by regression on positions from cheap self-play (k3 or the option B bot), described by what cards are (HP, type, costs, damage, effects) so it transfers, and run inside the Rust search, not called from Python per position. Measured by the table and by piloting a held-out deck. This is the sound core of run 6. It is not a policy network and not per-deck, and it comes after the cheap steps, not before.
7. **B4b merge from upstream as soon as the cards land**, with the rules checks re-run. Brews using B4b cards cannot be simulated until this is done, and the ladder is the only test for them in the meantime.

The deck loop stays as it is: Limitless says what is competitive, Dustin's taste says what is worth playing, the ladder decides. The screen's job stays "is this list clearly broken" (it called Payback right: 8% and 16% on the screen, 0 to 6 on the ladder). Once the pilot passes step 1 and step 3, the screen can also rank a brew's matchups against the eight lists as a prior for which brew gets Dustin's scarce ladder games. Dustin's ladder opponents are mostly not the tournament meta, so a brew that scores 40 to 45% against the eight lists can still be a ladder deck.

## 4. What not to do

- **No more per-deck or shared policy networks trained on who won, for now.** Runs 1 to 5 settled the card-number version; the card-description transfer question is open but is the most expensive question on the table, and section 8 gates it behind a cheap cloud probe. The Hydreigon run tonight is the last of the family; read it by the rule already set in `rl/RUN5.md` and do not spawn follow-ups from it whatever it says.
- **No run 6 build before steps 1 to 5 are done.** If run 6 happens, it is step 6: a scorer inside the search, judged by the table and a held-out deck.
- **No k7, no more depth tables.** Depth is not the lever; three tables have shown it.
- **No tuning against the 28 Limitless cells.** Twenty-eight noisy numbers (each real cell is ±5 to ±14 points) will be overfitted by any optimizer.
- **No new gates, launchers, seed ledgers or practice-run guards.** One line per job in the seed table is enough.
- **No bot number in a deck decision until the pilot passes step 3.** The ladder judges brews.

### What changed our minds tonight

- **The ladder record, read game by game.** 7-1 against homebrew piles, 5-10 against the eight panel archetypes (four of the five wins with Skarmory), 0-10 against other established decks; brews 7-17, Dustin's own decks 5-4; Lucario decks 0-5, Fire decks 0-4. Brews averaged three ladder games each against the 10 to 15 the loop asks for.
- **Option B's gain is pricing, not information and not Hyper Ray chipping.** The cloud attribution (branch `claude/pensive-ptolemy-spwc0b`, `rl/results/option_b_attribution_2026-09-24.md`) ran k3, b3n1, b3n4, b3o3n1 and b3o3n4 on the five worst cells, same deals. Blind k3 leaves unpriced any Trainer, attack or ability whose text names the opponent's hand or deck while those zones hold unknown cards: Copycat (in all eight lists), Mega Absol ex's Darkness Claw (its 80 damage counts as 0), Mars, Team Rocket's Boss. In 250 Hydreigon v Lucario games, Darkness Claw use went 76 to 255 and Copycat 214 to 457 once the guess filled the unknowns. - **It is a whole-deck lift, and the table is not a clean win.** On tonight's first eleven rows b3o3n4 is worse than k3 overall: average miss 9.3 to 9.7, and 5.7 to 7.5 on the eight rows outside the first look; favorites right 10 of 11 to 7 of 11. - **Two Sceptile cells may be data artefacts.** In the Sept 8 best-of-1-only reconstruction (36 events, 900 matches) Altaria v Sceptile is 33.7 (n 49) against 49.1 pooled and Sceptile v Vespiquen is 61.5 for Sceptile (n 26) against 33.1 pooled, both significant, and the pooled Vespiquen page records under half the matches other pages do. The other seven of nine beyond-chance misses reproduce in the same direction in the best-of-1 subset, so the big misses are not a series artefact.
- **The old target was unusable.** A perfect simulator expects 1.4 "pairings beyond chance" and produces 3 or more 16% of the time, the flag threshold tracks cell sample size (5.8 points at n 318, 14.5 at n 48), and a simulator that truly halved the error would pass the old target only 25% of the time. Replaced below.
- **Two new Altaria leads.** The threat clock treats Asleep or Paralyzed attackers as able to attack and nothing rewards Sleep, so Swablu's Sing is priced at 0, which would underrate Altaria everywhere (B2b). And the formula has no Weakness term at all (B3).

### Target, reading rules and process rules

- **PASS for the pilot, all three, confirmed on events after the freeze date:** (a) real error τ̂ = √mean[(S−L)² − SE_L² − SE_S²] at 5.5 or less (k3 11.1); (b) in every cell |S−L| − 1.96·√(SE_L² + SE_S²) at 10 or less (k3 fails five); (c) every deck's 7-opponent average within ±6 of Limitless (k3 fails four). Sceptile cells quarantined until B6. Simulated pass rates: a perfect simulator 97%, τ 4.0 77%, τ 5.5 43%, k3 0%.
- **Adoption of a new default pilot:** the new bot on the table's deals paired with the current one; ΔMSE = mean[(S_new − L)² − (S_old − L)²] with a bootstrap that resamples deals within cells and redraws every Limitless cell from Binomial(n, L); adopt only if the whole 95% interval is below 0; three vetoes: any cell's miss grows by more than 6 points, any deck's 7-opponent gap grows by more than 2, or any B2e held-out deck's panel average moves more than 2 points further from its Limitless value. The vetoes are applied to paired readings (per-game output, same deals); on an unpaired reading they are indicative only, because a pilot that changes nothing trips one 30 to 40% of the time at 500 deals (simulated on k3's table, 300 replicates: cell veto 0.38 and deck veto 0.31 unpaired; 0.01 and 0.03 paired at 500 deals; 0.00 and 0.00 paired at 1,000). The whole-table reading always reports each deck's 7-cell change, not only the scoreboard. Correlation, average miss, favorites right and pairings beyond chance are reported, never used to decide (their noise bands at 1,000 deals: ±0.04, ±0.6, ±2, ±2). Tonight's laptop table has only per-cell percentages, so its k3 vs b3o3n4 interval is an unpaired parametric bootstrap (conservative); from the cloud's first table on, `legality_scan` writes per-game output and every reading is paired by deal.
- **Variant screens (cheap vs expensive setting of the same idea):** the quantity is the real error τ̂ in points over the whole table with the Sceptile quarantine applied, paired by deal; the difference (cheap τ̂ minus expensive τ̂) gets its 90% interval from the same bootstrap as adoption; margin E = 3 points: interval entirely below E take the cheap one, entirely above take the expensive one, otherwise double the deals up to 2,000 per cell and at the cap take the expensive one only if its estimate is at least E. Never the five worst cells alone (they were picked partly on Limitless noise, so almost any change "improves" them); "cheapest within noise" is not a rule (a real 3-point gain reads as noise 78% of the time at 500 deals).
- **Process:** two review tiers (engine rules, the scoreboard tool, anything Dustin plays against and any pilot adopted get a second reader; diagnostics get none); every experiment starts with one sentence naming the decision it changes; no new launchers, guards, gates or ledgers; the laptop runs tables and A/Bs before any training; cloud sessions take no-laptop work onto branches for Dustin to take or leave; one session owns each results file and the others read it; every brew number carries its coverage flag.
